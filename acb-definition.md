Here is a rundown of how the Adjusted Cost Base (ACB) is calculated for Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) tax purposes. 

At its core, your ACB is the true "cost" of an investment. The CRA uses it as the baseline to figure out whether you made a profit or took a loss when you eventually sell an asset. 

Whenever you dispose of a capital property (like stocks, ETFs, mutual funds, or real estate), your tax liability is determined by this formula:

$$\text{Capital Gain (or Loss)} = \text{Proceeds of Disposition} - \text{ACB} - \text{Outlays and Expenses}$$

* **Proceeds of Disposition:** The amount you sold the asset for.
* **Outlays and Expenses:** Costs directly associated with selling the asset (e.g., trading commissions, legal fees).

Here is exactly how you arrive at the ACB figure to plug into that formula.

### 1. The Starting Point: Initial Cost
Your ACB is never just the raw ticker price of the asset. It is the purchase price **plus** any expenses incurred to acquire it. 
* If you buy 100 shares of a stock at $20 per share and pay a $5 trading commission, your total ACB is $2,005. 

### 2. The "Identical Properties" Rule (Average Cost Method)
If you buy shares of the same company or ETF at different times and at different prices, the CRA does not allow you to cherry-pick which specific shares you are selling. You must calculate a weighted average cost for the entire pool of those "identical properties."

* **Calculation:** Total cumulative cost of all shares divided by the total number of shares owned.
* **Example:** You buy 100 shares for $1,000. Later, you buy another 100 shares of the exact same stock for $1,500. You now own 200 shares with a total ACB of $2,500. Your new ACB per share is $12.50. 

### 3. Events That Increase Your ACB
A higher ACB is generally good news when it comes time to sell, because it shrinks your taxable capital gain. You must add the following to your ACB:
* **Additional Purchases:** Buying more units of the identical property.
* **Dividend Reinvestment Plans (DRIPs):** If your dividends are automatically used to buy more fractional or whole shares, you are technically taking cash and buying new units. This adds to your total ACB.
* **Phantom Distributions:** Common with ETFs and mutual funds, these are reinvested capital gains distributions. Because you are taxed on these distributions in the current year (usually appearing on a T3 slip), you add them to your ACB so you aren't double-taxed when you eventually sell the fund.
* **Superficial Losses:** If you sell a security at a loss but buy the identical security back within 30 days (before or after the sale), the CRA denies your ability to claim that loss immediately. Instead, the denied loss amount is added to the ACB of the repurchased shares.

### 4. Events That Decrease Your ACB
Certain events reduce your cost base, which will ultimately increase your taxable gain down the road:
* **Selling Shares:** When you sell a portion of your holdings, your *total* ACB drops proportionally, though your average ACB *per unit* remains exactly the same.
* **Return of Capital (ROC):** Common in mutual funds and Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs). If a fund pays you a distribution that is classified as a Return of Capital (Box 42 on a T3 slip), they are essentially giving you your own original investment money back. This is not taxed immediately, but you must subtract it from your ACB. 
* **The Zero Floor:** If ROC distributions ever drive your total ACB below $0, the negative amount must be reported as an immediate capital gain on that year's tax return, and your ACB resets to $0.

### 5. Corporate Actions
* **Stock Splits:** If a company does a 2-for-1 stock split, the number of shares you own doubles. Your total overall ACB remains identical, but your ACB *per share* is cut exactly in half. 
* **Consolidations (Reverse Splits):** The opposite applies. Your share count drops, your total ACB stays the same, and your ACB *per share* increases proportionally.

Tracking ACB can be a bit tedious, especially if you have a portfolio with DRIPs or frequent mutual fund distributions. However, keeping an accurate running tally ensures you aren't overpaying the CRA when it's time to cash out.
